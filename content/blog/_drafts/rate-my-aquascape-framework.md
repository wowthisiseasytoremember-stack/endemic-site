## Outline

- **Search intent / hook:** Hobbyist posts "rate my scape" and gets either flattery or unactionable nitpicks. Hook: "'Finished' isn't a feeling — it's five testable properties. Here's the framework, applied to two real community scapes with their actual flaws." Position Endemic as the evidence-based alternative to vibes-based critique.

- **Section 1 — Why most "rate my scape" feedback fails:**
  - The two failure modes: pure compliment ("looks great!") and undirected nitpicks ("maybe less of that plant") with no underlying principle.
  - What a critique framework gives you: a repeatable checklist you can run on your own tank before posting — self-critique first, community second.
  - Define "finished": not "no more changes possible" but "the composition holds under all five tests below."

- **Section 2 — The five-point framework (the core of the piece):**
  - **Balance:** visual weight distribution — tall hardscape vs. mass of plants, left vs. right. A heavy cluster on one side needs a counterweight (a mid-height plant group, a secondary stone), not necessarily symmetry.
  - **Negative space:** the empty water column and open substrate are part of the composition. Overplanted tanks read as cluttered; state the rough heuristic (a meaningful fraction of the frame should be open water, especially above the midline).
  - **Hardscape-to-softscape ratio:** hardscape sets the skeleton, softscape fills it — if plants fight the rocks for attention, one of them is wrong. Rule of thumb: you should still "read" the rock line through the planting.
  - **Depth/layering:** foreground/mid/background separation — height gradient from front (low carpet) to back (tall plants or rock peaks); flat silhouettes kill depth. Check from the front view only (that's what viewers see).
  - **Focal point:** exactly one dominant element (main stone, peak plant mass). Two competing focals = no focal point. Test: can a viewer name the first thing their eye lands on in under a second?

- **Section 3 — Case study A: the zen-garden pathway with the gap:**
  - Real post: a zen-garden-style scape where the "pathway" element leaves an awkward gap in the middle of the composition.
  - Run it through the five tests: the gap breaks negative space *intentionality* (reads as mistake, not design), weakens balance (empty middle pulls weight to both ends), and splits the focal point.
  - Fixes ranked by effort: (1) fill with a low mat plant to make the gap a deliberate band, (2) shift adjacent elements to close it, (3) reframe the photo so the gap sits at the edge. Lesson: gaps are fine when they're at the edges or clearly bounded — mid-composition gaps read as errors.

- **Section 4 — Case study B: the iwagumi that drifted into reds:**
  - Real post: an iwagumi layout that deviated from the classic green-minimalist form by leaning into red plants.
  - Run it through the five tests honestly: compositionally it may pass (focal stone intact, ratio held) while stylistically it's no longer iwagumi — call out the difference between *compositionally sound* and *genre-faithful*.
  - The useful reframe for readers: pick your genre constraints first (iwagumi = minimal, green-dominant, rock-led), then critique against those constraints. A red-heavy "iwagumi" isn't bad — it's just a different genre, and judging it by iwagumi rules produces false negatives.
  - Cross-link iwagumi-for-beginners for the canonical constraints.

- **Section 5 — How to use the framework (and when to stop):**
  - Self-critique workflow: photograph front-on, run the five tests in order, fix only one thing per week (plants grow; over-editing resets growth).
  - When "finished" is actually finished: all five tests pass AND the tank has been stable (no melt, no algae) for ~4–6 weeks. Before that, it's a work in progress — say so in the post title and get better feedback.
  - What NOT to optimize: chasing competition-level density as a beginner; the framework applies at any scale, including a 10 gal.
