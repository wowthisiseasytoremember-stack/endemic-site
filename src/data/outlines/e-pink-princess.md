## 1. SOURCE ARTICLE TEXT (from pillar JSON `narration_text`)

This is one of the most hyped houseplants of the last decade. There's a patent that covers its genetics. And there's a very good chance the one you bought isn't the plant on the paper.

The Pink Princess Philodendron rode a wave of social-media hype to become a genuine phenomenon. But underneath the aesthetics is a paper trail: US Plant Patent PP31149, protecting a specific genetic clone. So we ask a simple, uncomfortable question: when you buy a Pink Princess, are you getting the patented plant — or a clone that just resembles it?

A plant patent protects a specific asexually-propagated cultivar. Every legitimate copy is genetically identical to the original. The patent describes leaf shape, color, growth habit, the works. Crucially, it covers the genetics of one particular clone. It does not own the color pink. It does not own variegated philodendron. It owns that plant.

Here is where it gets messy. The market is flooded. Tissue-culture labs around the world pump out variegated philodendrons by the thousands, and the Pink Princess name gets slapped on a wide range of them. Some are the true patented clone. Many are simply similar-looking variegated plants sold under the same popular name. The name on the tag and the genetics in the pot are not guaranteed to match.

To investigate honestly, compare three things: the patent description, the seller's claim, and the plant in hand. Variegation is the tell. The famous pink coloring is unstable. It is a chimera, meaning the pink and green tissues are genetically different layers in the same plant. That instability is exactly why mass-produced clones drift — some revert to all green, some over-variegate and die. A patent describes an idealized specimen. The shelf delivers a lottery.

This is not about catching anyone red-handed. It is about informed buying. When you understand that the patent covers a specific clone, the market sells the name loosely, and the trait itself is genetically unstable, you stop expecting the tag to be a guarantee.

## 2. RESEARCH DATA PACK

### Patent Basics

A plant patent covers ONE specific clone, protects against unauthorized asexual propagation (cuttings/tissue culture for sale). Duration: 20 years from filing. Does NOT own the name "Pink Princess," the color pink, or variegated Philodendron as a category.

### Chimera Biology (L1/L2/L3 Model)

- **L1** (outermost): Produces leaf epidermis (skin)
- **L2** (middle): Produces photosynthetic mesophyll
- **L3** (core): Produces internal stem tissues

Pink Princess is a periclinal chimera: different layers have different genetics. One layer lacks chlorophyll → pink shows through transparent epidermis. Layer rearrangement during growth causes instability. Tissue culture multiplies this drift (each explant has different layer ratios).

### Three Types of Variegation

| Type | Mechanism | Stability | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chimeral** | Two genetic layers | UNSTABLE — drifts | Pink Princess |
| **Genetic** | Single mutation, all cells | Stable | Alocasia 'Macrorrhiza Variegata' |
| **Reflex** | Environmental (virus/nutrient) | Can revert | Mosaic symptoms |

### Buyer's Checklist

1. **Stem striping** — real PPP shows pink on stem. Solid green stem = reverted.
2. **Multi-leaf stability** — consistent marbling = stable. Sectoral (half-green/half-pink) = less stable.
3. **Leaf morphology** — compare to patent illustration.
4. **Etsy trap** — filtered photos vs. what you receive.
