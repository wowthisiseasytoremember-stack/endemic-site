## 1. SOURCE ARTICLE TEXT (from pillar JSON `narration_text`)

Pufferfish break most of the rules we set for community aquariums. They have teeth that never stop growing. They recognize their owners. Many are aggressive, most are messy, and a handful will kill anything you put beside them. And still, they remain one of the most beloved oddball groups in the hobby.

But behind every puffer in a glass box is a real place — a river system, a floodplain, a brackish estuary — with real water chemistry that shaped the animal you are now trying to keep alive.

This is a tour of eleven keepable species, ranked loosely from smallest to largest, each tied back to the water it actually came from.

First up, at barely an inch long, the Pea Puffer — also called the Dwarf or Indian Pygmy Puffer — is the gateway drug of the puffer world. It is fully freshwater, unlike many of its cousins, and comes from the slow, vegetated backwaters of Kerala's Pamba and adjacent river systems in southern India. Keep them in groups, heavily planted, and feed live or frozen — they will flatly ignore flake food. Watch for the classic puffer problem: they need snails to grind down their beak-like teeth.

Closely related to the Pea Puffer and often confused with it in the trade, the Dwarf Malabar Puffer variants share the same Western Ghats homeland — a global biodiversity hotspot of fast, tannin-rich streams tumbling out of the mountains.

Next, the Amazon Puffer. Here is the surprise: it actually schools. Unlike the lone-wolf temperament of most species, Colomesus asellus travels in groups through the whitewater channels of the Amazon. Fully freshwater, active, and comparatively peaceful, it is a fantastic species — provided you can supply the constant hard shells it needs to wear down its teeth.

The Red-Eye or Red-Tail Puffer is a small, gorgeous species from the peat-stained blackwater streams of Indonesia. Males flash a red tail and a keel along the belly during display. Soft, acidic water suits them best — a direct reflection of the tannin-loaded home streams of Borneo.

From the fast-flowing Mekong comes the Golden or Avocado Puffer, a torpedo-shaped, aggressive species built for current. It eats fruit and seeds in the wild.

Now the heavy hitters.

The Fahaka, or Nile Puffer, is an intelligent, utterly intolerant species that reaches well over a foot and was named by Linnaeus himself in 1758 — the oldest puffer name in science. It comes from the Nile and runs into a gill parasite problem in import that kills most of what arrives.

From the powerful Congo River, the Potato Puffer. This is the actual ambush predator of the puffer world. It buries itself in fine sand, leaving only upward-facing eyes exposed, and strikes prey that swims overhead. Give it deep, fine sand or don't keep it at all.

The Spotted Congo Puffer, by contrast, can be kept in groups — unusual for puffers — and its availability is war-tracked, with collecting affected by conflict in the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

The Mbu Puffer, also from the Congo and Lake Tanganyika, reaches 26 inches and needs a tank measured in hundreds of gallons, not tens. It is a decade-plus commitment to an animal with genuine personality.

Finally, three brackish species from the tidal transition zones of Southeast Asia — the Green Spotted, the Figure Eight, and the Ceylon Puffer.

The Green Spotted is the most tragically mistreated fish in the puffer family. Sold as a freshwater fish, it is in fact a brackish species that needs progressively higher salinity as it matures. It has one of the smallest known vertebrate genomes, sequenced in 2004.

The Figure Eight is more manageable — smaller, more tolerant of lower salinity. No two have quite the same pattern of loops and swirls, hence the name.

And the Ceylon Puffer is a robust, personable, somewhat pushy fish that rounds out the brackish trio.

Different waters. Different behaviors. Different rules. That's the point. Match the water to the origin, not to the tank that was already set up.

### Puffer Biology — Key Facts

**Teeth:** Polyphyodont — shed old bands, don't file continuously. Denying hard food = fused jaw = death.

**Toxins:** Bio-concentrated from bacteria in food chain. Captive puffers on pellets = non-toxic. Asian puffers = STX. Brackish/marine = TTX.

**Kottelat 2013:** *Tetraodon* is now African-only. Asian species split into *Dichotomyctere* and *Pao*. Stores still use old names.

**Smallest genome:** GSP at 340M bp. Sequenced 2004.
