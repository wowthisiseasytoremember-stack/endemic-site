## 1. SOURCE ARTICLE TEXT (from pillar JSON `narration_text`)

Almost every Anthurium in your collection — the velvet-leaf giants, the flamingo flowers, the rare crystallinum — shares one name in its scientific history: one Austrian botanist, one enormous genus, one forty-year career.

Heinrich Wilhelm Schott was an Austrian botanist who devoted his life to a single plant family, the Araceae, the aroids. If you keep houseplants, this is your family: philodendrons, monsteras, anthuriums, alocasias, pothos. And within it, Schott's fingerprints are everywhere. He is the primary describer behind an astonishing number of species, including the bulk of the genus Anthurium, the group at the heart of the modern rare-plant boom.

Schott's career centered on Vienna's imperial gardens at Schönbrunn, where he eventually served as director. This wasn't a dusty herbarium job. The imperial collections were fed by expeditions and a steady stream of living tropical plants arriving from the Americas, and Schott had access to them alive, growing, and flowering.

That access mattered enormously. Aroids are difficult to classify from dried specimens; their defining feature, the spadix and spathe, shrivels and distorts when pressed. Working with living plants let Schott see and document structures that other botanists, working only from dead material, simply couldn't.

Schott didn't just name plants. He organized them. He erected and defined numerous aroid genera, carving the sprawling family into workable groups. Many of the genus names houseplant people say every day trace back to his classification work. And Anthurium was his crown jewel. He is credited with describing well over a hundred species in the genus — a foundation so broad that when you look up the scientific name of nearly any Anthurium you own, you're likely to find Schott as the naming authority.

Like the great describers of his era, Schott understood that documentation was as important as discovery. He commissioned and oversaw a vast body of botanical illustration — detailed plates of aroid structures, beautiful and scientifically precise. These drawings were based on living specimens, which meant they captured anatomical details that would have been lost or distorted in a pressed herbarium sheet.

Much of Schott's living collection was lost after his death. Greenhouses close, plants die, gardens change. But his Icones, his botanical drawings, survived. They remain reference material for identifying aroids today because they were drawn from living plants that Schott had grown, studied, and understood.

The rare-plant boom of the last decade is a rediscovery of the very genus Schott spent his life mapping. The collector Anthuriums, the Instagram stars, the plants people pay hundreds of dollars for a cutting. His classifications are what let us talk about them. His structures are what let us distinguish one velvet-leaf from another.

(Schott) at the end of the name isn't a footnote. It's a signature from the director of the imperial gardens, who saw things in a living plant that no one working from dead pressed specimens could ever see.

### The Living Advantage

Aroids are defined by the spadix/spathe. Both shrivel and distort when pressed in herbarium sheets. Color fades. In living plants, both structures are fully visible. Schott's access to Schönbrunn's living collections gave him structural insights his peers couldn't match.

### The Lost Collection

Schott's living collection mostly died after his death. His botanical illustrations (*Icones*) survived. These are more valuable than herbarium sheets because they show living color and form. Drawn from specimens Schott had grown for years.

### Genera Shaped by Schott

Anthurium (crown jewel — 126+ species), Philodendron, Monstera, Alocasia, Colocasia, Dieffenbachia, Spathiphyllum — check authority on any of these: "Schott" or "(Schott)" appears constantly.

### Modern Connection

The rare-plant boom is a rediscovery of Schott's Anthurium work. The modern velvet-leaf obsession (clarinervium, crystallinum, warocqueanum, veitchii, regale) tracks the same trait Schott used for classification: leaf venation.

### Honorifics

*Schottarum* (genus of aroids named after him). *schottii* names. The Schott Archive at Natural History Museum Vienna.
