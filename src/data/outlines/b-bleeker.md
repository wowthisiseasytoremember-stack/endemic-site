## 1. SOURCE ARTICLE TEXT (from pillar JSON `narration_text`)

If you keep Southeast Asian fish, chances are one man named nearly half your tank — and he did it in his spare time, between shifts as a colonial army doctor.

Pieter Bleeker was not a professional ichthyologist. He was a physician, a medical officer for the Dutch colonial army stationed in what was then the Dutch East Indies, modern Indonesia. And in between treating soldiers and managing hospital wards, he described somewhere on the order of 1,100 new fish species, a number so vast that his name trails behind an enormous slice of the aquarium hobby's most familiar animals.

Here's the part that surprises people.

Bleeker didn't fund grand collecting expeditions. His genius was logistical. He built a network of local fishermen, market sellers, and colonial contacts across the archipelago who funneled specimens to him. Much of his material came straight from fish markets, the same stalls feeding the colony. A doctor with a scientist's eye, he understood that the daily catch was a rolling survey of regional biodiversity. Fresh specimens arrived, were sketched and measured, and slowly a picture of the entire region's fish fauna took shape, assembled not from the deep ocean but from dinner tables.

Bleeker's monumental work, the Atlas Ichthyologique des Indes Orientales Néerlandaises, is one of the most beautiful ichthyological works ever produced. He couldn't draw everything himself, so he commissioned local and Dutch artists to render specimens in extraordinary color while the fish were still fresh, capturing hues that vanish within hours of death. Those plates are why so many of his descriptions hold up today. When you preserve a fish in alcohol, it fades to grey. Bleeker's insistence on color-from-life gave later scientists something priceless.

Rasboras. Barbs. Gouramis. Loaches. Countless species that fill community aquariums today carry the authority abbreviation (Bleeker) after their scientific names. Every time you read a name like that, you're seeing a citation to a part-time naturalist working in a colonial hospital who saw something in a fish market that nobody had ever documented before.

Bleeker wasn't perfect. He worked at astonishing speed and some of his classifications were later revised. Duplicate descriptions, species reassignments, the usual noise of early taxonomy. But the scale of what he achieved, on his own time, with his own money, from a posting that most colonial doctors regarded as a hardship tour, was staggering.

He returned to the Netherlands in 1860, settled in The Hague, and spent the rest of his life publishing the Atlas and writing to ichthyologists across Europe. When he died in 1878, his collection of over twelve thousand specimens was bought by the Rijksmuseum in Leiden, where they remain today.

## 2. RESEARCH DATA PACK

### Full Biography

**Born:** 10 July 1819, Zaandam, Netherlands — son of a sailmaker, working class
**Died:** 24 January 1878, The Hague (age 58 — tropical diseases shortened his life)

**The origin story:** No formal university education. Apprenticed as a pharmacist. Self-taught in anatomy and zoology through the Haarlem museum library. Tried to get a job at the Leiden Museum — **rejected: "no vacancy."**

**Medical training:** Qualified as civic surgeon at 21. Six months in Paris (informal lectures). Passed military surgeon exam in 1842. Later received honorary doctorates from Leiden (1846) and Utrecht (1849).

**Why he joined the East Indian Army:** Rejected from the Leiden Museum. Needed a job. Still not inclined to settle as a practicing physician. The Indies was his escape route and his opportunity.

### The Political Context — Dutch East Indies 1842-1860

- **Cultivation System (Cultuurstelsel)** era — most extractive period of Dutch colonial rule
- Javanese villages forced to devote land to cash crops (coffee, sugar, indigo)
- Bleeker served as third-class military surgeon — treating soldiers dying of malaria, dysentery, typhoid, beriberi
- Joined the **Bataviaasch Genootschap** (Batavian Society of Arts and Sciences) — one of Asia's oldest learned societies
- **Founded the *Natuurkundig Tijdschrift voor Nederlandsch Indië*** in 1844 — his primary outlet
- Served as librarian of the Society, editor of the journal (22 volumes)

### The Banishment (1847-1849)

**Critical detail:** Bleeker was banished from Batavia for **public criticism of the high cost of postage** and his interest in population statistics. Colonial officials did not appreciate his interference. He was assigned to remote garrisons across Java — where he continued collecting fish.

### The Dokter Djawa School (1851)

He founded and directed this school at Batavia's military hospital — a two-year training program for indigenous medical assistants (taught in Malay). Genuinely progressive for the era.

### His Method

1. **Local fishermen network** — built relationships, daily catch from Batavia markets
2. **Government outposts** — contacts across Sumatra, Borneo, Java, Sulawesi, Moluccas
3. **Private museum** in his Batavia residence
4. **Correspondence**: Auguste Duméril (Paris), Albert Günther (British Museum)
5. Sold specimens to British Museum (131 in 1862, 367 in 1867)

### The Atlas Artists

- **L. Speigler** and **Ch. Engel** — primary illustrators
- Specimens painted **while still fresh** — color fades within hours of death
- Alcohol preservative turns fish grey — Bleeker's plates show living color
- 420 chromolithographed plates

### Collection After Death

~18,000 specimens bought by Rijksmuseum van Natuurlijke Historie, Leiden (now Naturalis Biodiversity Center). Type specimens still actively used in research (2021 Cirrhilabrus study).

### Aquarium Species He Described

The key table everyone cares about:

**Clown Loach** (*Chromobotia macracanthus* Bleeker, 1852) — his most famous. Large retractable spine below eye (hence *macracanthus* = large spine).

**Bala Shark** (*Balantiocheilos melanopterus* Bleeker, 1850) — black-tipped fins (*melanopterus* = black fin).

**Tinfoil Barb** (*Barbonymus schwanenfeldii* Bleeker, 1853) — common large community fish.

**Snakeskin Gourami** (*Trichopodus pectoralis* Bleeker, 1852).

**Kissing Gourami** — contributed to genus taxonomy.

**Many Rasboras** — foundation of the genus.

**Many Loaches** — genus *Pangio*, *Botia* contributions.

**Many Catfish** — dozens of siluriform species.

**Many Gobies** — including *Favonigobius reichei*.

**Pipefish/Seahorses** — *Microphis retzii*.

**Puffers** — some *Tetraodon* spp. (relevant to YOUR puffer content).
