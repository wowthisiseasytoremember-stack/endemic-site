## 1. SOURCE ARTICLE TEXT (from pillar JSON `narration_text`)

These aren't random words. Every scientific name is a tiny sentence — a dedication, a description, a place, or an inside joke hidden in Latin.

Scientific names look intimidating. They're not meant to be. Once you learn to read them, the label on the tank turns into a story. Broadly, a species name — the second word — falls into one of a few buckets: a description, a place, or a person.

Let's decode three real examples.

First, *exsul*, meaning "the exile." The Latin *exsul* means exile or banished one. It's used for species found in a strangely isolated spot — a fish cut off from its relatives, marooned in a single lake or spring. When you see *exsul* in a name, you're reading a note about geography: this one lives alone, far from its family.

Second, *davidi*, meaning "of David." The ending *-i* on a name almost always signals a person. *Davidi* means "of David." This is a patronym, a dedication honoring someone — often a collector, a sponsor, or a colleague of the describing scientist. The grammar even tells you who's being honored: *-i* means a single man, *-ae* means a single woman, *-iorum* or *-orum* means multiple people.

Third, *regani*, meaning "of Regan." *Regani* honors Charles Tate Regan, a towering British ichthyologist. Naming a species after a fellow scientist is one of the highest quiet compliments in the field — a permanent thank-you note written into the tree of life. Every time someone says the name, the honoree is remembered.

Put it together and the two-part name is doing two jobs. The genus, capitalized, tells you the group, the fish's close relatives. The species, lowercase, pins down which one — often by description, place, or dedication. So a name isn't a barrier. It's a compressed label written by the person who first described the animal, telling you what struck them most: where it lived, what it looked like, or who deserved the honor.

Exile. Of David. Of Regan. Once you can read them, every label in the fish shop stops being intimidating and starts telling you a story.

## 2. RESEARCH DATA PACK

### The Linnaeus System

Invented by Carl Linnaeus in 1758 (*Systema Naturae*, 10th edition). The starting point of all zoological nomenclature.

| Part | Job | Convention | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Genus** (1st) | Group/close relatives | Capitalized | *Caridina* |
| **Species** (2nd) | Which one | Lowercase | *davidi* |
| **Authority** | Who + when | (Bleeker, 1852) | *(Bleeker, 1852)* |

Parentheses around authority = species was originally described in a DIFFERENT genus from its current home.

### Suffix Rules — Complete

**Patronyms:** *-i* (single man), *-ae* (single woman), *-orum* (group), *-anus* (belonging to)
**Geographic:** *-ensis* (from), *-icus* (of), *-ianus* (belonging to)
**Descriptive:** Hundreds of Latin/Greek roots for colors, patterns, body parts
**Habitat:** *palustris* (marshes), *fluviatilis* (rivers), *lacustris* (lakes), *sylvestris* (forests)

### Pitfalls

1. **Names that lie:** *japonica* species can be from China or Korea
2. **Anthroponyms that look geographic:** *andreanum* = "of André," not "of the Andes"
3. **Kottelat revision:** Puffers renamed — old genera are obsolete
4. **Parentheses = history:** (Bleeker, 1852) means someone else reassigned it later
5. **Homonyms:** Same name, different species — taxonomic nightmares
