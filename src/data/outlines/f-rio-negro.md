## 1. SOURCE ARTICLE TEXT (from pillar JSON `narration_text`)

This water looks dirty. It is not. This is one of the cleanest, most specialized environments on the planet — and we are going to recreate it piece by piece.

The Rio Negro is the largest blackwater river on Earth, a vast tannin-stained tributary of the Amazon whose dark, acidic, mineral-poor water hosts some of the most iconic fish in the hobby: cardinal tetras, dwarf cichlids, and countless small characins. Recreating it is not about dumping in tea-colored water. It is about matching three pillars: substrate, light, and filtration and flow. Get those right and the biotope falls into place.

Pillar one is substrate. The floor of the Rio Negro is fine, pale sand, not rich aquasoil. This is a nutrient-poor system. The whole point of a blackwater biotope is that it is lean. Use fine light-colored blackwater sand or natural river sand as the base. Add a leaf litter layer on top — this is non-negotiable for authenticity. Leaves break down, release tannins, and host the microfauna your fish forage on. Add botanicals, seed pods, bark, and branches.

Pillar two is light. This is where most people go wrong. A Rio Negro tank should look dim and golden, not bright. In the real river, tannins absorb light and the canopy shades the water. Use lower intensity lighting, warm and amber in tone. Skip the high output planted tank lighting. The payoff: cardinal tetra colors pop against dark water and low light.

Pillar three is filtration and flow. Blackwater habitats are slow moving. The filtration goal is clean water with low flow. Keep the water clear without blasting the tank into a current. Accept that the water will stay tinted. Tannins are the goal, not a problem to filter out.

Aim for soft, acidic water. The Rio Negro runs at a pH of 4.5 to 5.5, near-zero hardness, and conductivity between five and thirty microsiemens. Total dissolved solids below forty parts per million. You match this with an RO system, remineralized lightly if at all, and you let the botanicals do the rest.

Time to wait. A biotope takes four to eight weeks to cycle — longer in blackwater because the low pH slows the bacteria. You will look at the dark tea-colored water, the bare sand, the leaves settling, and you will wonder if you made a mistake. You didn't. That ugly phase is the tank settling in. The bacteria establish, the tannins reach a stable concentration, the microfauna multiply in the leaf litter. The fish arrive and suddenly everything makes sense. The cardinal tetras school tighter in the dark water. The dwarf cichlids forage confidently through the leaves. The pencilfish hover at their characteristic angle, head down, scanning the surface.

It's not a dirty tank. It's the largest blackwater river on Earth shrunken down to glass.

### Rio Negro Water Chemistry (Scientific)

| pH | 4.5-5.5 | 3.5-5.0 |

**The blackwater paradox:** Lowest nutrients of any major river on Earth → highest biodiversity.

### Species Depth

**Cardinal Tetra:** NOT main river — floodplain tributaries and igapó forests. Structural coloration (guanine crystals) — not pigment. Group 10+ minimum. pH 4.5-5.5.

**Apistogramma:** *A. agassizii* (YES — Rio Negro). *A. macmasteri* (YES — Orinoco/Colombia). *A. cacatuoides* (NO — Peru/upper Amazon). Most are cave spawners.

**Pencilfish:** *Nannostomus eques* — head-standing behavior for surface prey ambush.

**Corydoras:** *C. pygmaeus*, *C. habrosus* — fine sand IS non-negotiable. Sharp substrate shreds barbels.
